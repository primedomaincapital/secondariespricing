GP-Led Secondaries: How Continuation Funds Price the Assets They Already Own

In the first half of 2026, GP-led deals passed $65 billion — and the price on the fastest-growing corner of secondaries is set by the same manager sitting on both sides of the trade. The checks exist. Whether they bite is the question.

By Prime Domain Capital Research  ·  November 19, 2026

The election packet arrived on a Tuesday, seventy pages thick, with thirty days on the clock. Inside it, a pension fund's investment staff found a price for an asset they already owned: a single company, held for nine years, moving from their fund into a new vehicle run by the same manager. The packet said the price was fair. A fairness opinion said so, in so many words. A new lead investor had already agreed to it. What the packet did not say, anywhere in seventy pages, was how the price had actually been set — who had bid against it, what it would have fetched on the open market, or what the manager stood to earn from the new vehicle if the staff signed. The staff had a month to decide whether to take cash or roll into a fund they had never chosen. The price was presented as a fact. It was, in truth, a negotiation they had not been invited to.

That is the central question in GP-led secondaries, now the largest half of a record market. Evercore's Private Capital Advisory counted $121 billion of secondary volume in the first half of 2026, up 19% year over year, with GP-led deals at $65 billion — up 35% — against $56 billion of LP-led volume. Jefferies counted $118 billion for the half, with GP-led deals at $62 billion, or 53% of the market, the first GP-led majority since 2021. Single-asset continuation vehicles are the engine: Evercore puts them at $34 billion in the half, up 88% year over year and 53% of all GP-led volume. The pricing question is no longer a footnote to this market. It is the market.

Three prices for one asset

A continuation fund price is never one number. It is three, and they rarely agree. The first is the net asset value — the manager's own estimate of what the company is worth, struck at a reference date, usually the last quarter-end. NAV is the starting point for every conversation, and the weakest anchor in it: the manager marking the asset is the same manager proposing to buy it. The second is the lead investor's price, negotiated bilaterally between the manager and the secondaries buyer putting up the largest cheque. That price is real money, and it is the number that ends up in the election packet. The third is the fairness opinion's range, produced by an independent valuation firm and expressed as a band the transaction price must fall inside. Each of the three answers a different question — what the manager thinks, what one buyer will pay, what an outsider will defend — and the election packet tends to present the second as if it were all three.

The headline numbers make the lead price look generous. In the first half of 2026, Evercore found 52% of single-asset continuation vehicle volume transacted at par and a further 14% above NAV, with buyers underwriting the deals to target gross multiples averaging about 2.3x, against roughly 1.7x for diversified LP portfolios. Jefferies' competing count puts LP portfolios at 87% of NAV on average for the same half, with venture stakes at 79%. The gap between the two prints is the story: the same market clears LP interests at a 13% discount and single-asset continuation vehicles at par or better. Either the assets are genuinely better, or the price is set by a process with fewer bidders and more interested parties.

Who checks the number

The checks on a continuation fund price are real, and they have just been rewritten. In October 2026 the Institutional Limited Partners Association published final guidance on continuation vehicles, replacing its 2023 version, after a draft in June 2026 that drew a four-month public comment period. The final document keeps the economics test that matters most: rolling LPs should end up no worse off than if the transaction had not happened, with fees and carry compared on a blended, risk-adjusted basis across all fund-level costs. On process, it softened. The draft wanted at least 30 business days for LPs to elect; the final settles on 20 clear business days, the same level as 2023. The guidance describes its own best practices as designed to guide, not dictate — and says a deal that falls short of them is not in itself a sign of a flawed process.

What the final guidance does insist on is evidence. The manager should demonstrate a fair market price through a competitive auction or other independent third-party validation. Where a fairness opinion is obtained, it should value the totality of the continuation vehicle's assets — not only the lead investor's stake — and disclose the valuer's scope, assumptions, fee arrangements, and any pre-existing relationships with the manager. The LP advisory committee should vote on the conflict, consulted early and reviewing the manager's choice of adviser, with terms reviewed no fewer than ten business days before signing. The law no longer forces the issue. The Securities and Exchange Commission adopted rules in August 2023 requiring registered advisers to obtain a fairness or valuation opinion in adviser-led secondary transactions; the Fifth Circuit vacated those rules, with the rest of the private fund adviser package, on June 5, 2024. No federal opinion requirement applies today. The fund agreement and the LPAC are the checks that remain.

“A fairness opinion prices the deal the manager designed, using data the manager supplied, on a timeline the manager set. It tells you the price is not indefensible. It does not tell you it is the best one available.”

Where the conflict pushes the number

The conflict is not a suspicion about continuation vehicles. It is the structure. The manager is the seller, setting the reference valuation and choosing the adviser; the manager is the buyer, sponsoring the vehicle that pays the price; and the manager is the beneficiary, earning fresh fees and carry on the new vehicle. The ILPA's 2023 guidance recommended the manager roll 100% of its carried interest into the continuation vehicle, explaining any departure — precisely because a manager that takes its carry in cash and re-earns carry on the same asset has been paid twice for one hold. Morgan Lewis's 2026 review of terms across 169 continuation vehicles found tiered carry in 79% of them. The price can be fair and the economics still extractive; the opinion covers the former, and nothing in the packet is obliged to cover the latter.

The most telling number in the market is not a price at all. It is the rollover rate — the share of existing LPs who choose to stay in. Jefferies' data, reported by PitchBook in 2026, puts the average rollover at 15% in 2025, up from 11% in 2023. A November 2025 working paper from the National Bureau of Economic Research, "Selling to Yourself: Continuation Funds in Private Equity," finds the opposite direction: the share of LPs rolling fell from 14% to 15% in 2018 and 2019 to below 5% by 2025, with public pension funds rolling at only 2% in 2024. The two measures count different things, and the gap between them is itself evidence of how little is standardised in this market. But the direction of the NBER finding is hard to ignore: if these are trophy assets priced fairly, with a fresh five-year runway, why do nineteen out of twenty investors take the cash? Part of the answer is mechanical. Many LPs must treat a roll as a new investment requiring full underwriting and investment committee approval, inside an election window measured in weeks. PitchBook's reporting quotes the reality plainly: for many LPs the election exists in name only, and in practice they must cash out. A price that cannot be accepted in the time allowed is not a real choice.

The case for the price

The defence of continuation fund pricing is strong, and the capital flows back it up. CVC Capital Partners closed its sixth secondaries fund at $10 billion in November 2026 — up from $5.8 billion in 2023 and $2.7 billion in 2019 — drawing commitments from more than 200 investors, under Carlo Pirzio-Biroli, who heads CVC's secondaries strategy. KKR closed its first private credit continuation vehicle in October 2026, more than $460 million for the loans of Lending Partners III, led by Allianz Global Investors; the $1.5 billion fund, raised in 2017, had returned an 11.0% net internal rate of return and 1.7 times capital by June 30, 2026. AllianzGI now leads or co-leads three of the year's credit vehicles, including a roughly $1 billion Blackstone credit continuation fund and Crescent Capital's $3.2 billion vehicle. Evercore counts $20.4 billion of credit secondaries in the first half of 2026 alone — more than in all of 2025.

The LP survey evidence points the same way. William Blair's Private Capital Advisory surveyed limited partners and LPAC members in July and August 2026 and found LPs support the liquidity optionality continuation vehicles provide by a margin of roughly two to one — 51% agree they are a net benefit to private markets, against 26% who disagree — with support more than doubling among LPs who had evaluated at least two continuation vehicles in the past 24 months. The same survey found 64% of LPs who quantified a rate reported that no continuation vehicle in their funds had seen the LPAC oppose, block, or force material changes, William Blair's own volume count runs higher than Evercore's $226 billion for 2025 — a reminder that every volume figure in this market is an adviser's estimate, not a tape. And the performance record holds up: a study by HEC Paris, led by Professor Oliver Gottschalg in partnership with Evercore, finds continuation vehicle returns broadly comparable to buyout benchmarks, with a narrower spread for single-asset vehicles. The concentrated bet, diligently chosen, performs. The alternative to a continuation vehicle is often worse — a forced sale into a thin market, or a fund extension that leaves the manager running an asset with no economics at all.

What a rolling LP should demand

None of that makes the price in any given packet fair. It makes the structure legitimate and the individual deal a matter for diligence. The negotiation that matters is not whether to roll. It is the terms of the roll, and the leverage sits in four demands. First, an independent valuation commissioned by the LPAC, not the manager — valuing the totality of the assets, with the valuer's assumptions and fees disclosed, as the ILPA's final guidance now expects. Second, a longer election window written into a side letter before the deal arrives, since 20 clear business days cannot underwrite a roll that needs investment committee approval. Third, a carry holiday or reduced carry on the rolled capital, tested against the ILPA's no-worse-off economics test on a blended basis — the rollover should not pay a second full carry on an asset it already owned. Fourth, a status-quo option priced honestly enough to be a real choice: stay in the original fund on its original terms.

The market will keep setting records either way. Continuation vehicles were roughly 14% of sponsor-backed exits globally in 2025, up from 5% in 2021 (Jefferies data cited by ILPA) — a mainstream exit route, no longer a tail-end fix. Dedicated secondaries dry powder stood at about $194 billion at mid-2026, down roughly 10% from about $215 billion at the start of the year, with the capital overhang now close to 1.0x, according to Evercore. But discipline at the market level is no substitute for diligence at the deal level. The price in the packet was set by someone with an interest in where it lands. The LP's job is to find out who else agreed with it, what they were paid to agree, and what the number would have been if the manager had been only the seller — or only the buyer — instead of both.
