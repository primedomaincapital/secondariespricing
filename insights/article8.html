Venture Secondaries vs. Buyout Secondaries: Why the Discounts Are Not Comparable

A venture stake at 79% of NAV and a buyout stake at 92% are not the same discount wearing different clothes. The NAV means something different in each, the buyer pool is different, and the discount signals something different.

By Prime Domain Capital Research  ·  November 19, 2026

The investment committee had two offers on the table. One was a portfolio of buyout fund interests at 92 percent of net asset value. The other was a portfolio of venture fund interests at 79 percent. The venture discount was bigger, the venture return target was higher, and the venture portfolio looked, on the numbers, like the better trade. It was not — or rather, it was a different trade entirely, and the committee's mistake was treating the two discounts as if they measured the same thing. They do not. A discount to NAV means one thing when the NAV is a buyout fund's audited holdings and another thing entirely when it is a venture fund's mark on a company that has never turned a profit. The 13-point gap between 92 and 79 is not a pricing inefficiency. It is a different asset wearing the same label.

The secondary market has grown into the closest thing private markets have to an observable price, and the price it reports is a percentage of NAV. In the first half of 2026, LP portfolios sold at an average of 87 percent of NAV, according to Jefferies — but the average is the least informative number in the report. Buyout portfolios cleared at 92 percent. Venture and growth cleared at 78 to 79 percent. Credit held at 91 percent. Real estate lagged at 70 percent. The spread between the best and worst strategy is 22 points, and it is not noise. It is the market pricing four different kinds of NAV, four different buyer pools, and four different risks — and calling all of them "the secondary discount" is like calling every temperature on a weather map "the weather."

What the NAV actually is

The first difference is what the NAV measures. A buyout fund's NAV is built from companies with revenue, earnings, and comparable public multiples. The GP marks the portfolio quarterly, the marks are audited, and a buyer can diligence them against real cash flows. A venture fund's NAV is built from companies that are, in many cases, pre-revenue, pre-profit, and valued by their last financing round — a round that may be two years old, set by an investor with a different agenda, and stale the moment the market turns. When a venture stake trades at 79 percent of NAV, the buyer is not buying a 21 percent discount on a verified number. The buyer is buying a 21 percent discount on a number that is itself an estimate, and the real discount — measured against what the companies are actually worth — may be larger or smaller. Nobody knows, which is the point.

That uncertainty is why the buyer pools are different, and the buyer pool is what sets the price. Buyout secondaries have the deepest buyer pool in the market: dedicated secondary funds, evergreen vehicles, pension funds, and now retail channels, all competing for the same interests. Dedicated dry powder stood at approximately $194 billion at midyear 2026, and the capital overhang multiple — available capital divided by trailing twelve-month volume — is now close to 1.0x, meaning there is roughly one dollar of dry powder for every dollar of annual volume. That competition compresses the buyout discount. Venture secondaries have a thinner pool: fewer buyers, more specialization, and a meaningful share of the market that simply will not touch venture at any price, because the NAV is harder to verify and the path to distributions is longer. The 2022 rate shock showed the difference in its purest form: LP pricing fell to about 81 percent of NAV overall, but buyout held at 87 percent while venture fell to 68 percent. The same shock, two different markets.

The distribution problem

The second difference is the distribution clock, and it is the one sellers underestimate most. Buyout funds distribute. Venture funds promise to distribute. Ropes & Gray's secondaries update, published in September 2026, put annual distribution yields near 10 percent of NAV in the first half of 2026, against a long-term historical average of approximately 25 percent. Bain counts distributions at 14 percent of NAV in 2025, the fourth straight year below 15 percent. That drought hits venture harder than buyout, because a venture fund's distributions are concentrated in a small number of exits — one IPO, one acquisition — while a buyout fund's distributions come from a portfolio of companies that generate cash every quarter. A buyer of a venture stake at 79 percent is buying a longer wait for a lumpier return, and the discount compensates for the wait, not just for the risk.

Fund age sharpens the point. Funds younger than five years averaged 95 percent of NAV in 2025 — effectively par — while tail-end funds older than ten years averaged 73 percent. The venture discount is, in part, an age discount: venture funds stay in the market longer, their remaining NAV is smaller relative to the original commitment, and the tail-end interests trade at the deepest discounts of all. Evercore cites about 70 percent of NAV for tail-end interests since 2022; PitchBook puts the discount at 35 to 45 percent for stakes eight-plus years old. A venture stake that looks cheap at 79 percent may be a tail-end stake that is cheap for a reason, and a buyout stake that looks expensive at 92 percent may be a young fund that is fairly priced. The strategy label tells you less than the vintage does.

What the discount signals

The third difference is what the discount signals, and this is where the comparison breaks down completely. A buyout discount of 8 percent usually signals liquidity: the seller needs cash, the buyer provides it, and the discount is the price of immediacy. A venture discount of 21 percent can signal liquidity, or it can signal distress — a fund whose best companies have already exited, leaving a portfolio of also-rans — or it can signal structure, a NAV that was never realistic in the first place. The same number means three different things, and the buyer who does not know which one is buying blind. The target returns tell the story from the other side: buyers underwrite venture secondaries to about 21.7 percent IRR and 2.15x gross multiple, against 17.0 percent and 1.62x for buyout, according to Setter Capital's H1 2026 report. The higher return target is not a free lunch. It is the price of the uncertainty.

There is a counterargument, and it is worth stating plainly: the venture discount has compressed, and the buyers who treat it as permanent are missing the trade. Venture and growth pricing recovered to 78 percent in 2025, its sharpest improvement in years, from the 68 percent low of 2022. Positions written off as unsaleable in 2023 now clear respectably. The recovery is real, and it is driven by the same forces compressing buyout discounts — record volumes, with Evercore putting first-half 2026 at approximately $121 billion, up 19 percent year over year, and full-year volume tracking toward $250 to $260 billion against $226 billion in 2025. But the recovery does not make the discounts comparable. It makes the venture discount smaller, not the same. A venture stake at 79 percent and a buyout stake at 92 percent are still different assets, and the 13-point gap is still the market's judgment about which NAV it trusts.

A seller weighing the two should ask three questions. First, what is the NAV made of — audited cash flows, or a financing round from two years ago? Second, how old is the fund, and how much of the NAV is tail-end? Third, what is the distribution clock — quarterly cash, or a single exit that may never come? The answers determine whether the discount is a liquidity premium worth capturing or a warning worth heeding. And the buyer should remember the one number that flatters every headline price: deferred payment terms, which Jefferies estimates lifted headline prices by about 400 basis points in 2024. A venture stake quoted at 79 percent with a deferred payment is not the same as a buyout stake quoted at 92 percent in cash. The discount is not the price. The price is the price, the terms, and the NAV — and only the first of those three is printed on the cover.
