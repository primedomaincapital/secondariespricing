Discount or Premium? Reading the NAV Spread

Two funds, same vintage year, sold in the same quarter — one clears at 108% of NAV, the other at 62 cents. What fund quality, vintage, manager, and unfunded commitments tell you about secondaries pricing.

Prime Domain Capital  ·  October 2026

Picture a secondaries investment committee on a Tuesday morning. On the table: two portfolios, both 2020-vintage buyout funds, both offered at reference-date NAVs set by their GPs within weeks of each other. The team bids 108% of NAV for the first — a premium — and walks away from the second at 70% because the price still isn't cheap enough. Same vintage year. A 38-point spread between conviction and rejection.

This is the NAV spread, and learning to read it is the entire discipline of secondaries pricing. The spread is not noise around a fair value. It is the market's verdict on everything the NAV doesn't tell you.

What the spread is actually pricing

Start with the uncomfortable truth: NAV is an opinion. A GP marks its portfolio quarterly, and those marks reflect the manager's judgment — sometimes conservative, sometimes aspirational — about private companies with no observable price. Every bidder in a secondary process re-underwrites the portfolio from scratch. The discount or premium that results encodes five things at once.

Fund quality comes first. Top-quartile buyout funds with strong DPI histories — actual cash returned, not paper marks — clear at the tightest levels. In 2025 and early 2026, top-quartile buyout interests from 2019–2022 vintages were clearing at 92–94% of NAV, a 6–8% discount, according to market clearing data consistent with the Jefferies and Lazard reviews. Broad-market buyout of similar vintage cleared 10–15% off. Growth equity ran 15–25% off. Venture, 20–35%. Tail-end funds more than ten years old: 25–45% off, and in the worst cases steeper still. One buyer's underwriting desk sees a portfolio of profitable, sponsor-backed businesses with line-of-sight to exit. Another sees a venture book marked at cost-plus-hope. The NAV looks similar on paper. The price is not.

Vintage is the second dial. Younger funds carry more blind-pool risk — capital not yet deployed, companies not yet proven — and price wider. Older funds offer visibility but bring the J-curve's mirror image: the remaining assets are often the ones that didn't exit, and buyers discount accordingly. The sweet spot in recent years has been the 2017–2021 vintages: seasoned enough to underwrite, young enough to still hold upside.

The manager matters more than the model admits. A GP with a history of conservative marking earns tighter pricing because buyers trust the reference number. A GP known for aggressive marks gets punished — the buyer simply haircuts the NAV before bidding, which shows up as a wider "discount" that is really a credibility adjustment. Lazard's interim 2026 secondary market report found pricing becoming "increasingly sector-dependent," with software facing sharper scrutiny as investors placed less reliance on sponsor-marked NAVs. Translation: when buyers stop trusting the marks, the spread widens.

Unfunded overhang — the capital the LP still owes the fund — is the quiet killer of pricing. An interest with 40% unfunded is not just a portfolio; it is a forward commitment to keep wiring money into a blind pool. Buyers price the obligation, and sellers with heavy unfunded balances routinely accept discounts 5 to 10 points wider than fully funded equivalents. Mostly deployed vintages often price tighter than their quality alone would suggest: there is simply less left to fund.

The 2024–2026 repricing

The trend across the last three years has been compression at the top and bifurcation below. Average LP-led pricing rose from 85% of NAV to 89% during 2024, per Evercore's data, and hit 90% in the first half of 2025, per Jefferies — a steady climb from the 2022 lows, interrupted only briefly by tariff-driven volatility in April 2025. Private credit secondaries tell the extreme version of the story: GP-led credit transactions averaged 98% of NAV in 2025, according to Evercore's 2025 Credit Secondary Market Survey, with some deals clearing at par or better.

The GP-led market shows the split most vividly. Lazard's interim 2026 report found that 23% of single-asset continuation fund volume priced above par in H1 2026, up from just 8% in 2025 — trophy assets commanding premiums as GPs moved their best companies into continuation vehicles. Multi-asset continuation funds went the other way: the share pricing below 90% of NAV jumped to 40% from 16% a year earlier, as investors demanded sharper discounts for complexity. Quality up, complexity down. The market is learning to discriminate.

What are buyers underwriting to justify these prices? Dedicated secondaries funds have historically targeted net IRRs of 12–16% at the median, with top-quartile managers delivering 16–22% over the past decade. And the engine of those returns is not the discount. Hamilton Lane's secondary market research found that roughly two-thirds of secondary returns come from post-purchase appreciation — the portfolio growing after the buyer takes over — rather than from buying cheap. The discount is downside protection. The underwriting is the return.

The nuance the bulls skip

Here is the counterargument, and it deserves a straight hearing. A rising pricing tide can float stale marks. When average LP pricing climbs from 85% to 90% of NAV in eighteen months, part of that move is genuine — more bidders, healthier portfolios. Part of it is mechanical: GP marks drifted upward through 2024 and 2025, so buyers pay a higher percentage of a higher number. If the marks are soft, today's tight discounts are tomorrow's impaired returns wearing a tighter spread.

The data gives the skeptics ammunition. When 23% of single-asset continuation vehicles clear above par, some premiums are paid for assets the GP chose to keep — the classic adverse-selection worry. The GP knows the asset best; the premium bidder is betting the GP's optimism is justified. Sometimes it is. Dispersion in outcomes is widening even as average pricing tightens — exactly what you would expect when the best assets get bid up and the rest get left behind.

Reading the spread forward

Treat the NAV spread as a map of information quality and the next few years come into focus. The top of the market — recent-vintage, top-quartile buyout, conservative marks, low unfunded — will keep pricing toward par and beyond it. Buyer competition is structural: dedicated dry powder near $200 billion does not sit idle, and the largest secondaries houses — Lexington Partners, Coller Capital, Ardian, Blackstone Strategic Partners, Hamilton Lane — are raising ever-larger funds that must be deployed. That capital clears at the top.

The bottom of the market is where the real money gets made, and where the real risk lives. Tail-end funds, venture books, heavy unfunded commitments — these clear at 60 to 75 cents because few buyers can underwrite them and fewer sellers can wait. For the specialist willing to do the work, the spread is the opportunity.

The implication is that "the" secondary price no longer exists, if it ever did. There is a price for quality and a different price for everything else, and the distance between them is growing. The NAV spread is the market's way of saying what it cannot print on a tape: not all private equity is the same asset, and it never was.
