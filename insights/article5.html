The Second Exit Has a Second Price Tag

Single-asset continuation vehicles now run 54% of GP-led volume, and 87% of that volume priced above 90% of NAV in the first half of 2026. The deal looks like a win for everyone. The fee schedule says otherwise — the rollover pays carry twice.

Prime Domain Capital  ·  November 2026

The LP committee had thirty days. That was the window to decide whether to roll a decade-old position into a continuation vehicle or take cash and walk away. The fairness opinion said the price was fair. The lead investor had signed. The presentation showed the asset’s growth, the new buyer’s conviction, the target multiple. What the presentation did not show, on any slide, was the new fee clock: a fresh 20% carry, running from a lower cost basis than the one the original investors had actually paid, on an asset they already owned.

That is the second price tag on the second exit. The secondary market’s boom is real — the volume figures below are records by any measure — but the boom’s flagship product quietly restarts the meter for the very investors it claims to serve.

The boom, in one paragraph

Estimates differ at the margin, which is itself a sign of how fast the market is moving. Evercore’s Private Capital Advisory counted $226 billion of secondary volume in 2025; Lazard counted $233 billion; Jefferies roughly $240 billion. The first half of 2026 set another record: Evercore measured $121 billion, up 19% year over year, with GP-led deals at $65 billion, up 35%, against $56 billion of LP-led volume. Lazard measured $124 billion, up about 28%, with trailing-twelve-month volume of $260 billion — double the 2021 market — and a full-year forecast near $275 billion. Call it $118 billion to $124 billion for the half, depending on whose count you use. Either way, it is the strongest first half on record, and GP-led deals are now roughly half the market.

Inside the GP-led half, concentration is the story. Single-asset continuation vehicles represented the largest share of GP-led activity, at 54% of GP-led volume in the first half of 2026, with continuation funds overall accounting for 84% of GP-led deployment. Pricing has proved resilient: the majority of single-asset volume transacts at par, another 14% clears above NAV, and Lazard found 87% of single-asset continuation fund volume priced above 90% of NAV in the first half of 2026, with the share pricing above par nearly tripling to 23%. Jefferies counts an average continuation vehicle size of about $900 million, twenty-nine GP-led deals above $1 billion in 2025, and roughly 80% of the top 100 sponsors by assets under management having completed at least one continuation vehicle.

The Savant case

The human version of the trend arrived this year, when Hamilton Lane led a $270 million single-asset continuation vehicle so Cynosure Partners could keep its decade-old stake in Savant Wealth Management, now roughly 25 times larger than in 2016. The logic is easy to admire: a good asset, a manager that knows it, new capital to keep growing it. The structure is easy to miss: the investors who roll are not extending their original fund. They are entering a new one, with a new hurdle rate, a new preferred return calculation, and a fresh 20% carry — and, in a growing share of deals, super-carry above it. Morgan Lewis’s 2026 review of terms across 169 continuation vehicles found tiered carry in 79% of them. The GP keeps the asset and resets the fee. The LP keeps the asset and pays again.

The fee reset

The arithmetic is what the LP committee’s thirty days are really for. Target gross multiples average about 2.3x for single-asset continuation vehicles, against about 1.9x for multi-asset vehicles, 1.8x for single LP interests and 1.7x for diversified LP portfolios, according to Evercore. That premium is the GP’s case for the structure: concentrated, high-conviction assets should earn more. But the multiple is measured from the continuation vehicle’s cost basis, not from the price the original LPs paid a decade ago. The return on the second ride is real. So is the second carry. An LP that rolls without modeling both is not making an investment decision. It is renewing a subscription.

The case for the rollover

None of this makes continuation vehicles a bad deal. The counterargument is strong, and the data supports it. A study by HEC Paris and Evercore finds continuation vehicle returns broadly comparable to buyout benchmarks, with a narrower spread for single-asset vehicles — the concentrated bet, diligently chosen, performs. When 23% of single-asset volume prices above par, the market is saying the assets are worth more than the reported NAV, not less. And the alternative to a continuation vehicle is often worse: a forced sale into a thin market, or a fund extension that leaves the GP managing an asset with no economics at all. The continuation vehicle solved a real problem — funds that outlived their legal life but not their assets — and the market’s growth is the evidence that the solution works.

The honest version of that defense has a condition attached, and it comes from the Institutional Limited Partners Association’s 2023 guidance: the GP sits on both sides of the trade, so the safeguards are an LPAC conflict vote, independent price validation, and a 30-day minimum decision window for LPs. The guidance exists because a fairness opinion and a lead investor, on their own, are not sufficient — the lead investor’s economics are not the rolling LP’s economics, and the opinion is about price, not about fees.

What LPs should demand

The negotiation is not about whether to roll. It is about the terms of the roll: a longer decision window than thirty days, an independent valuation the LPAC actually commissions, a carry holiday or a reduced carry on the rolled capital, and a status-quo option — stay in the original fund on its original terms — priced honestly enough to be a real choice. The market will keep setting records either way. The question is whether the investors who built the market keep any of the record for themselves, or pay it away in a second carry on assets they already owned.
