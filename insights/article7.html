How Long Does a Secondaries Sale Take From Mandate to Close?

Distributions are running near 10% of NAV against a long-run average near 25%, and sellers want out. A clean sale takes eight to fourteen weeks — if nothing goes wrong, and if the GP’s calendar cooperates.

By Prime Domain Capital Research  ·  November 19, 2026

The chief investment officer wanted the cash by June. It was January, and the distributions had stopped behaving like distributions. Ropes & Gray's secondaries update, published in September 2026, put the number on it: annual distribution yields were holding near 10% in the first half of 2026, against a long-term historical average of approximately 25%. The CIO's staff had a buyer in mind, a portfolio of seasoned buyout interests, and a calendar that assumed the sale would behave like selling a stock. It does not. The buyer would need weeks. The lawyers would need weeks. And the general partner — the manager whose consent the sale could not close without — would need whatever time the fund's calendar allowed, on a schedule the seller did not control.

The honest answer to how long a secondaries sale takes is a range, and the range is the point. A well-prepared single fund interest typically completes in eight to fourteen weeks, according to Breslin's guide to selling an LP interest, with the general partner's consent usually setting the pace. Premier Alternatives, a buyer-side advisory, puts a straightforward single-interest sale at sixty to one hundred twenty days from engagement to close. Setter Capital's volume report for the first half of 2026, which surveys buyers directly, says a seller-assembled portfolio runs a competitive auction typically over eight to twelve weeks. A portfolio of interests, a venture-heavy book, or anything with a complicated fund document runs longer. Yantacaw Capital's venture liquidity guide stages it by structure: an LP secondary sale takes one to three months, a GP-led strip sale two to four months, and a tender offer three to six months. None of those figures is a promise.

The calendar, stage by stage

A sale moves through five stages, and each has its own clock. The first is preparation: reading the limited partnership agreement, assembling the file, and picking a reference date. The LPA controls the sale the way a company's bylaws control a share sale — transfer article, consent procedure, rights of first refusal, transfer windows, minimum sizes. The price is struck as a percentage of net asset value at a reference date, usually the last reported quarter-end, so the quarter-end sets the rhythm before anything is marketed. A clean file is the difference between a price indication in days and one in weeks.

The second stage is the auction. The adviser circulates the portfolio to a short list of specialist buyers — Hamilton Lane, Lexington Partners, Coller Capital, Ardian, Blackstone Strategic Partners — who bid against the reference NAV. In the first half of 2026, pricing was firm for quality: Setter Capital's survey puts LP-led fund sales at an average of 89.1% of NAV and GP-led deals at 94.8%, while Jefferies' count puts LP portfolios at 87% of NAV on average, with buyout funds at 92% and venture and growth funds at 78%. The third stage is diligence, where the winning bidder confirms what the NAV actually contains. The fourth is documentation: a purchase and sale agreement, a transfer agreement, and the consent request to the manager, typically submitted around weeks five and six in a buyer-side timetable. The fifth is the consent itself, and then the close — usually timed to a quarter-end so the capital accounts cut over cleanly. The transfer of the interest, from the manager's notice to the closing, typically takes four to eight weeks on its own, according to Carta's guide to LP transfers, with the timeline varying by fund structure, GP requirements, and know-your-customer complexity.

Why GP consent sets the pace

Every stage can be hurried except the one that matters. Nearly all limited partnership agreements require the manager's written consent before any transfer can occur, and most give the manager discretion to approve or deny it. This is not a formality. The manager uses consent to control who becomes an LP in its fund, and a buyer the manager already knows clears consent faster than a stranger. Buyer guides put the consent wait at fifteen to thirty business days after a formal transfer request, with the timetable running weeks six to ten of the process — but that is the cooperative case. Some process transfers only at quarter-end, with deadlines for submitting notice. Some charge a transfer fee, or require counsel review at the seller's expense. Carta's guide notes that standard transfers used to cost $5,000 to $15,000 in fees and take four to six weeks to close, but in recent years the cost has risen sharply, with some firms now charging upwards of $60,000 for a single transfer. And a manager can simply say no. A price on an interest the manager will not consent to transfer is not a price.

The rights sitting alongside consent are what turn a delay into a derailment. Some funds carry a right of first refusal in favour of the manager or the other investors, who may match the buyer's price and take the interest themselves. Setter Capital's survey of the first half of 2026 found that when deals break, the leading reason — in 72% of failures — is simply the seller deciding not to sell, with rights of first refusal exercised in 13.9% of failures. Only 12.2% of buyers saw more deals fall through than in the prior period, so the market is not collapsing mid-process; it is stalling at the gate. The ROFR clock runs on the fund's timetable, not the seller's, and a manager that wants the interest for its own book has every incentive to let the clock run. Add multi-jurisdictional compliance or a subscription line that needs the lender's sign-off, and the eight-to-fourteen-week sale becomes a six-month one.

The price of speed

Time and price trade against each other in this market, and the trade is visible in the terms. About a third of LP-led deals in the first half of 2026 included deferred consideration, according to Ropes & Gray's summary of the half — most settling within twelve months — and Setter Capital's survey puts the split at 73.6% of deals paid entirely in cash on closing against 22.3% partially deferred. Deferral is how a seller defends a headline price: Jefferies estimates deferrals lifted headline prices by about 400 basis points in 2024. The seller gets to report 92 cents on the dollar; the buyer gets to pay part of it next year, out of distributions the asset itself generates. FundCount's guide to private equity secondaries, published in October 2026, makes the same point from the buyer's side: about one-third of LP-led deals now include deferred payments, which lets sellers defend a headline price. The deferred slice is not free money. It shifts timing risk to the seller, and it flatters the price the market reports — which is why the 87% and 89.1% averages from Jefferies and Setter Capital, counting different samples on different bases, should be read as ranges around a negotiation, not as a tape.

The same trade shows up in the discount itself. The October 2026 print that made the point most sharply was Blackstone Strategic Partners' purchase of part of a $1.3 billion portfolio of 22 co-investment stakes, reported by PitchBook at about 20% below net asset value — roughly ten points below Jefferies' buyout print for the half. Co-investment stakes sell for less than fund stakes, and a $1.3 billion book still cleared at eighty cents. A seller that needs cash by June has one lever the patient seller does not: it can accept the discount, the deferral, or the buyer the manager already likes. The calendar is a negotiating position, and most sellers discover that only after they have spent it.

“A price indication comes in days. A closing comes in quarters. The gap between the two is where secondaries sales are won, lost, and repriced.”

The case for the slow lane

It is easy to read all of this as obstruction, and that reading misses half the story. The consent process exists because the remaining LPs have an interest in who joins their fund: a buyer that cannot fund future capital calls is a liability to every other investor, and the manager is the only party positioned to judge that. The quarter-end discipline exists because capital accounts, fee calculations, and distribution waterfalls all cut over on quarter boundaries; a mid-quarter close is an accounting exercise nobody wants to pay for. The ROFR exists because the manager and the existing LPs built the portfolio, and a right to match the price is a right to keep the asset in friendly hands. Even the transfer fee, inflated as it has become, pays for real work: know-your-customer checks, anti-money-laundering review, tax documentation, and a fund administrator that must re-paper the position. A market that cleared in two weeks would be a market with no diligence at all — and the discounts in that market would be worse, not better, because buyers would price the risk they could not check.

The counterargument has numbers behind it, too. Dedicated secondaries capital fell to $290 billion in the first half of 2026 from $327 billion at the end of 2025, according to Ropes & Gray, with total available capital down to $328 billion from $477 billion at the end of 2025 — the buyer universe is deploying faster than it is raising, and the capital overhang is thinning. The sellers who complain loudest about consent often chose the cheapest adviser or a buyer the manager had never heard of. The process is slow because the asset is complicated, the counterparties are regulated, and the documents were written by people who had seen what happens when transfers go wrong.

How sellers buy back weeks

The weeks are recoverable, mostly before the mandate is signed. Read the LPA before anything else, and confirm the consent procedure, the ROFR, the transfer windows, and the minimum size — a sale that fights the LPA does not close, and a sale designed around it does. Assemble the file before the adviser is hired: the fund's holdings, the unfunded commitment, the distribution history, the side letters, the last four quarters of NAV. Pick the reference date deliberately, and time the launch so the reference NAV is fresh and the close lands on a quarter-end. Involve the manager early, at the right moment — not so early that the auction is compromised, and not so late that consent becomes a surprise — and bring a buyer the manager already knows. The buyer-side timetables all agree on the sequence: legal review of the LPA in week two, purchase agreement and consent request in weeks five and six, consent in weeks six to ten. A seller that arrives at week one with the LPA already read and the file already built has bought back the first month. A seller that arrives with neither has donated it.

The market's scale makes the discipline worth it. Secondary volume reached $121 billion in the first half of 2026 alone, according to Evercore, up 19% year over year, with full-year 2026 tracking toward $250 billion to $260 billion against $226 billion in 2025. A GP-led process takes longer still: two to four months for a strip sale, three to six for a tender, on Yantacaw's staging. The LP weighing a sale should plan on ninety days, budget for six months, and judge the adviser on whether the consent request lands in week five rather than week nine. The CIO who wanted cash by June and started in January was not wrong about the market. The market was there, the buyers were there, and the price was roughly eighty-seven cents on the dollar. The CIO was wrong about the calendar — and in secondaries, the calendar is the only part of the deal the seller cannot reprice.
